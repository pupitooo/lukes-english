What changed and why

Tenses

  • I knew I need → I knew I needed: after a past tense (knew) the next verb moves into the past too.
  • he run back → he ran back: run is irregular (run, ran, run).
  • Before he run back, I started yelling → After he ran back, I started yelling: first he ran off, then you yelled.
  • I made it easy and tell them → I kept it simple and told them: both verbs must be in the past.
  • they know they need → they had to; wasn’t clear, we could: reported speech moves back in time.
  • before I start to be furious → before I lost my temper: “start to be” isn’t used; say get furious or lose my temper.
  • all of them was → all of them were; I leads → I lead: the -s belongs only to he/she/it.
  • I was yealed something → I yelled something: you don’t want the passive here.

Words and phrases

  • directly ahead of us means “in front of us, going the same way”. You wanted straight towards us.
  • call at it → call out to him. Yell at / shout at means shouting angrily at someone.
  • him / it / its / their: a dog can be he, she or it, but don’t mix them. Here it’s he, him, his.
  • started to play / started playing: both are correct.
  • shortly means “soon”. For “a little while” use briefly or for a short while.
  • also goes before the verb (He also tried…); at the end of a sentence use too.
  • at least as tall means “as tall or taller”, the opposite of what you meant. Use at most as tall or no taller than.
  • lost his attention → lost interest.
  • pissed → pissed off; much more → even more; more angry → angrier.
  • say anything → say something (anything is for negatives and questions).
Version 2

Just happened

Telling someone fresh news. Present perfect (I’ve just taken, it hasn’t gone) announces what happened and why it matters now. Once you give the details, switch to past simple. Signal words: just, still, yet, already, never.

You won't believe what has happened! I've just got back from my bushcraft club with the kids, and we've had a run-in with a dog without a leash. We were on our way back from our secret spot when I noticed him running straight towards us. I know a lot about dog behaviour, so I knew I needed to get his attention. I called out to him and played with him for a short while. He tried to sniff my kids too, but they were all smaller than him, so I kept standing between them and him. After a while he lost interest and ran back to his owners. I've never been so angry at dog owners! I've just yelled at them: "What is this supposed to mean? You can't control him! Where's your leash?" They haven't said a word. That has made me even more pissed off. I told them that they have to control their dog, and that if anything isn't clear, we can call the cops. I've just walked away from them, because I didn't want to lose my temper.

Version 3

A period that isn’t over yet

The “I’ve had a crazy week” pattern. Present perfect describes a time that is still running: this week, today, so far. Everything counts towards the present moment, so the whole story stays in present perfect.

I've had a stressful week. Every afternoon I've led my bushcraft club for kids, and nothing has gone wrong so far. Until today! Today a dog has run straight towards us on our way back from our secret spot. I know a lot about dog behaviour, so I've called out to him and played with him for a short while. He has also tried to sniff my kids, but I've kept standing between them and him. Finally he has lost interest and run back to his owners. I've never been so angry at dog owners! I've asked them, "Where's your leash?" and "Why can't you control him?", but they haven't said a word. That has made me even angrier. I've just told them that they have to control their dog, and I haven't calmed down yet.

What changed and why

Tenses

  • what has happend → what happened: the story is told in the past, and “one day” calls for past simple.
  • the bus hasn’t been there yet → the bus still hadn’t arrived: it’s the past, so past perfect.
  • We go to school at 7:23 → We had to be at school at 7:23: past tense, and “had to” expresses the obligation.
  • they don’t look like they wanted → they didn’t look like they wanted: both parts in the past.
  • it was a short trip → it was supposed to be a short trip: a plan that didn’t work out.
  • A fast walk takes at least 15–20 minutes: present simple is fine here, it’s a general fact.

Words and phrases

  • bus station is a bus terminal. A stop is a bus stop.
  • economic is about the economy. For “cheap and cost-saving” use economical or just cheap.
  • on fresh air → in the fresh air (or outdoors).
  • by their own → on their own.
  • go to the school → go to school: no article when it’s about attending school. “The school” is the building.
  • took a car sounds like a taxi or a rental. Your own car: took the car or went by car.
  • Articles were missing in the bus, the other kids, a short trip, a fast walk.
  • 15-20m → 15–20 minutes; m could mean metres.

A period that isn’t over yet

The “I’ve had a crazy week” pattern. Present perfect describes a time that is still running: this week, today, so far. Everything counts towards the present moment, so the whole story stays in present perfect.

I've had a crazy week. Every morning I've taken both of my kids to school by bus, and the plan has always been simple: a short trip, then a short walk back home in the fresh air. But this week the bus hasn't arrived on time even once. Today I've waited at the bus stop for twenty minutes and it still hasn't come. We have to be at school at 7:23, so we've almost run out of time. I've looked at the other kids, and nobody has decided to walk. A fast walk takes at least 15-20 minutes, so we've finally given up and taken the car. I've never seen so many parents driving to school! I haven't asked anyone what they have done yet.
